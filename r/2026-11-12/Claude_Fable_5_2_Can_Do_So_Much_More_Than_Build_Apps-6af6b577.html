Mark turns an M5Stack StackChan desk robot into Lil Token, a voice assistant that checks his calendar, drafts email, researches questions and reacts to touch and music. His point is that AI coding tools can build playful physical projects, not just apps. He says it took 20 versions: Fable 5.2 handled planning and the first builds, while Opus 5.5 helped finish the iterations.

Key ideas

What it does: Lil Token speaks in Mark's cloned voice, shows which service it is using, reacts when picked up or patted, and changes expressions and dances with music. Mark says it checks for his return with a camera snapshot every 20 seconds, with those camera checks and recordings kept on his Mac. This is not an entirely offline system: Claude, voice synthesis and connected services are also involved. 1:21

The hardware: Mark says he paid $99 for the robot, choosing it for its mix of screen, camera, microphones, speaker, sensors, motors and Wi-Fi. He replaced its original software rather than keeping the manufacturer's cloud assistant. The $99 is his reported hardware price, not the total project cost. 2:49 chapter start

Creator's key callouts

Eight-part build prompt, in the creator's order. The prompt walkthrough begins at 4:00; the steps below combine captions with on-screen viewing notes.

Items covered (not ranked)

Identify the hardware

Have the agent read the exact model's maker documentation, product page, existing firmware projects and owner discussions. Mark uses Firecrawl to research the parts and what can be changed.

youtube.com

Back up, then connect

Make a full software and firmware backup before wiping anything. He uses the phone app, Mac iPhone Mirroring and computer control to configure Wi-Fi, then works directly between the Mac and robot.

youtube.com

Build the assistant

Put one Claude agent on the Mac in charge of the robot through the Claude SDK. Add Firecrawl for research, Google tools for email/calendar and Composio for other apps. His voice output uses Fish Audio's Drama 3 model; he says he supplied audio from 15 earlier videos to clone his voice.

youtube.com

Wake, then listen

Use Hey Lil Token as the wake phrase and allow roughly 10-15 seconds for the request, so pauses do not cut the user off.

youtube.com

Keep answers short

Specify brief, natural spoken replies. Add bracketed emotion directions to the voice output so reactions sound excited, startled or dizzy rather than flat.

youtube.com

Require approval

Let it read and draft, but require permission before sending, posting, refunding or buying. Mark specifically says it must not send email by itself.

youtube.com

Trace failures

Keep error logs and second-by-second screenshots during tests so the agent can compare intended behavior with actual faces, tool indicators and reactions.

youtube.com

Let the Mac test it

Play synthetic questions through the Mac's speakers into the robot and have Claude inspect the conversations. Mark reports more than 30 simulated conversations without him manually speaking each request.

youtube.com

Caveats

The build was not one-shot: Mark describes slow replies, missed touch/wake commands, wrong tool calls and motors loud enough to keep the robot dancing after music stopped. Logs and repeated tests drove the fixes. 8:07 chapter start
